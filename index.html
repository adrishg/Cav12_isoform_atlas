CACNA1C · CaV1.2 · L-type calcium channel

One gene, one channel, dozens of channels

CaV1.2 is the L-type calcium channel that lets calcium into excitable cells when the membrane depolarises. It is what dihydropyridines block, what couples excitation to contraction in heart and smooth muscle, and what carries a large share of activity-dependent calcium signalling in neurons. One gene, CACNA1C, encodes it — a single α1 subunit of four homologous domains, six transmembrane segments each, that folds into one pore.

The complication is that the gene is spliced into many different channels. Roughly a fifth of its exons are alternative, and the combinations differ between heart, smooth muscle and brain, between fetal and adult tissue, between species, and — measurably — between individual people. So “CaV1.2” in a paper rarely means the same protein as “CaV1.2” in the next one. This atlas is the map of which is which.

Read this before using anything else

Four things the databases will not tell you

This atlas exists because the CaV1.2 splicing literature contains three systematic traps. Each one has produced published errors. Each one is unavoidable if you work from a single accession page.

1. “Canonical” does not mean physiologically dominant — at either the sequence or the transcript level

The UniProt canonical human sequence Q13936-1 (2,221 aa) was cloned from fibroblasts, not heart (Soldatov 1992, PMID 1316612) — and GTEx records CACNA1C at 0.031 in cultured fibroblasts versus 10.65 in left ventricle, a ~340× difference. Separately, the MANE Select transcript NM_000719.7 carries the minor, smooth-muscle-enriched IS6 exon, not the heart- and brain-dominant one. Neither “canonical” label describes the channel in the tissue you are probably studying.

2. Two groups use opposite labels for the same pair of exons

The mutually exclusive IS6 exon pair is named in two incompatible ways that are both in active use. The heart- and brain-dominant exon is called exon 8 by Splawski and the clinical/Timothy-syndrome literature, and exon 8a by the Soong lab and much of the splicing field. The smooth-muscle-enriched exon takes the other label in each system. Papers describing the same developmental switch report it with opposite exon names. Use the toggle in the header to pick which system leads; the other is always shown in grey beside it. The only unambiguous anchor is the genomic coordinate.

3. The human canonical sequence is a composite — it contains both members of both mutually exclusive pairs

Read the VAR_SEQ coordinates of Q13936 carefully and something odd appears. Exon 21 sits at residues 932–951 and exon 22 at 952–971 — adjacent, both present. Exon 31 sits at 1297–1324 and exon 32 at 1325–1352 — likewise. These are mutually exclusive exons: no real transcript carries both. The displayed canonical carries both pairs, and the arithmetic confirms it — remove one 20-residue and one 28-residue exon from 2,221 and you get 2,173, within two residues of the rabbit cardiac clone's 2,171.

The consequence: the 2,221-residue human sequence is a reference scaffold, not a protein that exists in any cell. Anyone computing a molecular weight, running a structural alignment, mapping peptides, or numbering a residue from it is working from a composite. This is not a UniProt error — a canonical sequence has to display every alternative segment somewhere — but it is rarely stated, and it silently shifts residue numbering downstream of each pair.

4. Gene-level expression says nothing about which isoform is present

GTEx and the Human Protein Atlas quantify the locus. None of their numbers identify exon 1a/1b/1c, 8/8a, 9*, 21/22, 31/32 or 33 usage, and the Ensembl transcript IDs they quantify against do not map cleanly onto the literature's exon nomenclature. On the tissue map these two kinds of evidence are kept in separate boxes and are never combined into a single number.

The shorthand used on every page

One notation, seven slots, always in the same order. It follows the convention the splicing literature already uses — the smooth-muscle isoform written as 1/8/9*/32/Δ33 — with two deliberate departures noted below.

Two departures from the original notation. Soong and colleagues write the smooth-muscle N-terminus as plain 1; this site writes 1b, because a third variant (1c) predominates in resistance arteries and “1” cannot distinguish them. And exon 9* is written with an explicit + or − rather than by presence or absence of the token, so that a slot is never silently empty. Slots left unspecified by a source are omitted; the Sequence checker writes ? where it could not make a call.

Evidence tiers used throughout

Every isoform, tissue assignment and functional claim on this site carries one of these. Hover any tier badge for the reason it was assigned.

TIER 1

mRNA and protein in native tissue

Transcript detection plus an isoform-discriminating antibody, mass spec, or knockout-validated protein assay, in tissue rather than a cell line. Rare. Only exon 1, exon 9*, and the e21+22 aberrant variant reach this bar.

TIER 2

Transcript evidence only

RT-PCR, qPCR, colony sequencing, RNA-seq, long-read, single-cell. The default state of this field. Says the mRNA exists; says nothing about whether the protein is made.

TIER 3

Heterologous construct or inferred

HEK293/oocyte expression of a designed cDNA, or a claim asserted from prior work without direct tissue evidence. Nearly all CaV1.2 electrophysiology is Tier 3 — including the biophysics of the named “tissue” isoforms.

DISPUTED

Papers actively contradict each other

Applied on top of a tier, not instead of it. Both sides are cited on the Disputes tab. Four loci currently carry it.

Accession note

Human entries frequently linked as “the CaV1.2 page” include Q5V9X9 — a 210-residue TrEMBL fragment submitted as “splice variant 9,” with both termini flagged non-terminal. It is a real record but a partial one. This atlas anchors on Q13936 (CAC1C_HUMAN, reviewed, 2,221 aa, 37 annotated isoforms), with P22002 (rat, 2,169 aa) and P15381 (rabbit, 2,171 aa) as orthologues.
